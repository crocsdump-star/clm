Translating Phrases to Mathematical Expressions

Mathematics is a language — words become numbers, variables, and operators.

➕ Addition phrases

plus · increased by · added to · more than · combined with · sum / total of

➖ Subtraction phrases

minus · decreased by · diminished by · reduced by · subtracted from · less than · the difference of · exceeded by

✖️ Multiplication phrases

times · multiplied by · product of · of (e.g. "1/2 of a number")

➗ Division phrases

divided by · the quotient of · the ratio of · the fraction of · per

⚠️ Watch out: "greater than" is a comparison symbol (>), not an operation word — don't confuse it with "more than," which usually signals addition.

Worked translations

"5 more than a number" → x + 5
"a number decreased by 8" → x − 8
"7 less than a number" → x − 7 (order flips!)
"the product of a number and 6" → 6x
"the quotient of a number and 4" → x ÷ 4
"twice a number diminished by 3" → 2x − 3

Polya's 4-Step Method of Problem Solving

A universal framework for solving any word problem, math or business.

Step 1 — Understand the Problem

What is being asked? What are the given facts? Find the connection between what you know and what you need.

Step 2 — Devise a Plan

Write the number sentence. What operations must be performed? Choose a strategy: Guess & Check, Look for a Pattern, Draw a Picture, Use a Model, Work Backwards, Use a Formula, Solve an Equation, or Use Direct Reasoning.

Step 3 — Carry Out the Plan

Execute your plan step by step. Check that each step is correct before moving to the next.

Step 4 — Look Back

Verify your answer makes sense. Did you use all the given information? Is the answer labeled correctly (units, PhP, %, etc.)?

Example: Mount Everest is 29,028 ft above sea level; the Dead Sea is 1,312 ft above sea level. Find the difference.
1. Understand: asked = difference in elevation.
2. Plan: Difference = Highest − Lowest.
3. Solve: 29,028 − 1,312 = 27,716 ft.
4. Look back: Both elevations are positive and Everest is clearly higher — answer is reasonable.

🔄 Flashcards

✍️ Try It Yourself

Work through each problem using all 4 steps, then reveal the full solution.
1. A vendor sold 45 items on Monday and 12 more items on Tuesday than Monday. How many total items were sold over both days?
1. Understand: asked = total items sold both days.
2. Plan: Tuesday = Monday + 12; Total = Monday + Tuesday.
3. Solve: Tuesday = 45 + 12 = 57. Total = 45 + 57 = 102 items.
4. Look back: 102 is reasonable since Tuesday alone is already more than half.
2. A delivery van starts the day with a full tank. It uses 3/8 of the tank in the morning and 1/4 of the tank in the afternoon. What fraction of the tank is left?
1. Understand: asked = fraction of tank remaining.
2. Plan: Remaining = 1 − (3/8 + 1/4).
3. Solve: 1 − (3/8 + 2/8) = 1 − 5/8 = 3/8 of the tank.
4. Look back: 3/8 + 5/8 = 1 whole ✓.
3. Room temperature was −2°C at dawn. It rose 9°C by noon, then dropped 6°C by evening. What is the evening temperature?
1. Understand: asked = final evening temperature.
2. Plan: Final = −2 + 9 − 6.
3. Solve: −2 + 9 = 7; 7 − 6 = 1°C.
4. Look back: Starting negative, rising more than it drops overall → a small positive result makes sense.

Operations on Integers

The number line, absolute value, and sign rules.

Absolute Value

The distance of a number from zero — always non-negative. Symbol: | |. Example: |−3| = 3.

➕ Addition Rules

SignsRuleResult Sign
Same signsAdd the numbers, keep the common signsame as both
Different signsSubtract (larger − smaller absolute value)sign of the larger |value|

➖ Subtraction Rule — "Keep, Change, Change"

  1. Keep the minuend (first number)
  2. Change subtraction to addition
  3. Change the sign of the subtrahend (second number)
  4. Then apply the addition rules above
3 − (−2) → 3 + (2) = 5

✖️➗ Multiplication & Division Rules

SignsResult
Same signs (+×+ or −×−)Positive
Different signs (+×− or −×+)Negative

Exponents — quick reminders

  • (−2)⁴ = 16 (the negative is inside the parenthesis, raised too)
  • −2⁴ = −16 (only 2 is raised; the minus applies after)
  • Any number to the power 1 equals itself: 6¹ = 6
  • Any (non-zero) number to the power 0 equals 1: 5⁰ = 1

Order of Operations — PEMDAS

Parenthesis → Exponents → Multiplication/Division (left to right) → Addition/Subtraction (left to right)
  1. Parenthesis — solve inside grouping symbols first
  2. Exponents — simplify powers
  3. Multiplication / Division — whichever comes first, left to right
  4. Addition / Subtraction — whichever comes first, left to right
Example: 2 + (4 − 5) × 3
= 2 + (−1) × 3  (parenthesis)
= 2 + (−3)  (multiplication)
= −1  (addition)
Example: −4 ÷ (2 + 2) × 5 − 3
= −4 ÷ 4 × 5 − 3  (parenthesis)
= −1 × 5 − 3  (division, left to right)
= −5 − 3 = −8

Remember: multiplication doesn't always come before division — do them in the order they appear, left to right. Same for addition/subtraction.

Operations on Fractions and Decimals

Fraction types

  • Proper: numerator < denominator (3/4)
  • Improper: numerator ≥ denominator (7/4)
  • Mixed number: whole number + proper fraction (1 3/4)

Add / Subtract Fractions

Similar (same denominator): just add/subtract numerators, keep the denominator.

Dissimilar (different denominators): find the LCD (Least Common Denominator), convert to equivalent fractions, then add/subtract numerators.

a/c ± b/c = (a ± b)/c   |   a/b + c/d = (ad + bc)/bd

Multiply Fractions

Multiply numerator × numerator, denominator × denominator. Cancel common factors first to simplify.

(a/b) × (c/d) = (a×c)/(b×d)

Divide Fractions

Keep the first fraction, flip (reciprocal) the second, then multiply.

(a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (d/c)

Decimals — Add/Subtract

Align the decimal points vertically. Add trailing zeros as placeholders, then add or subtract normally.

Decimals — Multiply

Multiply as whole numbers (ignore decimal points), then count the total decimal places in both factors and place the decimal point that many places from the right in the product.

1.25 × 4.3 → 125 × 43 = 5375 → 3 total decimal places → 5.375

Decimals — Divide

Move the divisor's decimal point right until it's a whole number; move the dividend's decimal point the same number of places. Then divide normally.

1.24 ÷ 0.2 → 12.4 ÷ 2 = 6.2

Equity Ownership & Fractional Expense Ratios

Applies fractions to business ownership, profit shares, and cost allocation.

Key idea

Equity ratios define ownership distribution among partners. All partner shares always sum to 1 whole (100%).

Formulas

Partner C's Fraction = 1 − (Partner A's Fraction + Partner B's Fraction)
Partner's Cash Share = Total Net Profit × Partner's Equity Fraction
Department's Overhead Share = Total Overhead × (Department Space ÷ Total Space)
Example: Partner A holds 2/5, Partner B holds 1/3. Total profit = PhP 450,000. Find Partner C's cash share.
C's Fraction = 1 − (2/5 + 1/3) = 1 − (6/15 + 5/15) = 1 − 11/15 = 4/15
Cash Share = 450,000 × 4/15 = 30,000 × 4 = PhP 120,000
Example: Total space = 500 m². Production = 250 m². Rent = PhP 100,000.
Production's fraction = 250/500 = 1/2 → Rent = 100,000 × 1/2 = PhP 50,000

🔄 Flashcards

✍️ Try It Yourself

Solve using Polya's method, then reveal the full worked solution.
1. Partner A owns 3/10 of a firm, Partner B owns 2/5. If total net profit is PhP 300,000, what is Partner C's equity fraction and cash share?
C's Fraction = 1 − (3/10 + 2/5) = 1 − (3/10 + 4/10) = 1 − 7/10 = 3/10
Cash Share = 300,000 × 3/10 = PhP 90,000
2. An office of 600 m² is shared by three departments: HR uses 120 m², IT uses 180 m², and Operations uses the rest. Monthly rent is PhP 90,000. Find each department's fractional share and rent.
HR: 120/600 = 1/5 → Rent = 90,000×1/5 = PhP 18,000
IT: 180/600 = 3/10 → Rent = 90,000×3/10 = PhP 27,000
Operations: 600−120−180=300 → 300/600=1/2 → Rent = 90,000×1/2 = PhP 45,000
Check: 18,000+27,000+45,000 = 90,000 ✓

CAPEX, OPEX & Financial Statement Ethics

Capital Expenditure (CAPEX)

Funds used to acquire, upgrade, or maintain long-term physical assets (property, buildings, equipment, technology). Capitalized on the balance sheet, not expensed immediately — benefits extend beyond one tax year.

Examples: buying a new warehouse, purchasing production machinery, building a data center, office buildout.

Operating Expenses (OPEX)

Day-to-day costs of keeping the business running. Fully consumed within the current period and deducted immediately from revenue on the Income Statement.

Examples: salaries, rent, utilities, SaaS subscriptions, advertising, office supplies.

OPEX Sub-categories

  • COGS (Cost of Goods Sold) — expenses directly tied to production (raw materials, direct labor).
  • SG&A (Selling, General & Administration) — expenses to run the business overall (rent, marketing, admin salaries), regardless of production volume.

Financial Statement Ethics — 5 Core Principles

  • Integrity — honest, no misrepresenting or omitting facts
  • Objectivity — report based on evidence, free from bias or conflict of interest
  • Professional Competence & Due Care — stay updated, avoid negligent errors
  • Confidentiality — protect sensitive data, prevent insider trading/leaks
  • Professional Behavior — comply with laws, avoid discrediting the profession

Ethical practice examples

  • Full disclosure of liabilities/lawsuits in footnotes
  • Accurate revenue recognition (only after delivery/service rendered)
  • Fair valuation of assets (updating inventory/investment values honestly)
  • Giving auditors unrestricted access to records
  • Whistleblower protection channels
